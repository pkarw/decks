## 01. It's time to Buidl

It's time to Buidl. Piotr Karwatka, entrepreneur and investor, co-founder of Open Mercato and Catch the Tornado. Made in Wrocław, VC Demo Day, 27 October 2026, 11:15–11:45.

## 02. I like to build

I like to build. I have built freeware, software distributed on CDs, websites, eCommerce businesses and companies. Between 2000 and 2006 I worked on freeware, including the Zajączek HTML editor + 20 other programs. During the Web 2.0 era, from 2005 to 2010, I built Blogfrog.pl and Znam.to. Then came Divante, from 2008 to 2022, with a PLN 260 million valuation at exit, and Callstack, from 2016 to 2025, with a PLN 500 million valuation at exit. I also invest: thirty companies, including ElevenLabs. The common thread has always been building. Source: personal history, dates, valuations and investment count supplied by Piotr Karwatka for this keynote. The figures describe company valuations at exit, not personal proceeds.

## 03. IT wasn’t always sexy

IT was not always sexy. A lot of us were here because we loved technology and making things. In 2006, in my first job as a programmer, I earned USD 800. And I was incredibly happy. The attraction was the chance to create something, to solve a problem and to see it work. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s personal account supplied for this keynote. ## 04. IT is losing its appeal again

Now IT is losing its appeal again, at least in the sense we have understood it over the last ten years. A lot of people feel disappointed. The expectations they built around the industry no longer match what they see happening. I understand that feeling. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 05. A single prompt

You used to earn a lot as a developer. Now you can see a single prompt produce a better result. That can be an uncomfortable experience when technology and technical work have been central to your identity. In many areas, the technology itself is losing value. This is the change I am reacting to as a builder. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 06. Sometimes, that gets me down

Sometimes, that gets me down. Smile and let the thought land. It is an honest reaction to seeing the value of familiar technical work change so quickly. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s own account supplied for this keynote.

## 07. But mostly, it gets me excited

But the much bigger feeling is excitement. I cannot remember the last time I was this excited about the possibilities for building things. There are ideas I can now attempt that I would previously have put aside. That is what keeps bringing me back to building. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s own account supplied for this keynote.

## 08. I’m building again

I am building again. I invest much less now, even though I see many more investment opportunities than before. That is a deliberate choice about where I want to spend my time. Right now, I believe it is a good time to build. I want to explain what brought me back to building and what I think this means for founders in this room. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s own account and opinion, supplied for this keynote.

## 09. AI Engineering

AI engineering lets you build the things you have been thinking about for years, including ideas you may once have thought were beyond your reach. That is how I started Open Mercato. Along the way I learned a huge amount. The learning happened through building: making decisions, seeing the result and improving it. ## 10. A working MVP

The cost of building software is lower, so you can keep building for much longer before you need an investor. That changes what I expect from a founder approaching me. Today, asking for investment without a working MVP is hard for me to take seriously. Show me something that works. It can be narrow and imperfect, but it should let us have a concrete conversation about what you are building and what you have learned. This is my view as a software founder and investor. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s position supplied for this keynote.

## 11. What should you build?

What should you build? Something people need. I see two distinct paths. One is a local unicorn: Poland is a large market, costs are low, and AI can be a tool for building the product. Your moat is go-to-market, sales and integrations. Time can work in your favor as the software gains depth. The other path is VC-backed frontier AI: building technology at the cutting edge. That is extremely difficult and capital-intensive. The cases in between, such as building another Viktor or a Grok bot, are also very hard because competition is enormous. These are my views on the opportunities, supplied for this keynote.

## 12. Dev tools

Where does AI’s role go unquestioned? Dev tools. While building Open Mercato, we developed our own way of working with AI engineering. Those experiences became Cezar, our tool for coordinating coding agents, and the AI Tech Leaders program. We turned what we learned while building into products, tools and practices we use ourselves. The point is that a product can grow directly out of a problem your own team has experienced. Sources: Piotr Karwatka’s narrative supplied for this keynote. ## 13. Infrastructure

Infrastructure is another opportunity. SaaS products and local integrations can provide the services AI uses to solve concrete business problems. The value may sit in a capability that an agent can call, or in connecting to systems a company already depends on. The business still needs a specific result. Think about which part of that workflow you can provide reliably as a service. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s thesis supplied for this keynote.

## 14. Business knowledge as a moat

Build products that fit your customers better and contain a great deal of business knowledge. Gathering that knowledge and encoding it in the product creates a substantial moat against competitors. It is also work that many teams do not want to take on. The depth comes from understanding how the customer’s business actually operates and making the product reflect it. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 15. Delivery economics

Some models were difficult to make work when technology was expensive. Lower building costs can change those economics. Fixed-price work and waterfall delivery are examples I would reconsider. Fixed price describes a commercial model, and waterfall describes a delivery approach, so these are different kinds of choices. Neither becomes automatically successful because AI is available. The opportunity is to reassess models that previously looked unattractive and see whether the new cost structure gives you an advantage. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s thesis supplied for this keynote.

## 16. The VC commitment

Remember that a large VC round comes with a commitment. Business models are changing faster than ever. Having one design partner and popularity driven by hype may mean other teams will arrive very soon. Those early signals do not mean you have a durable advantage. So where might other teams be less eager to go? Boring business problems that someone genuinely wants to pay you to solve. That is where I would look for concrete demand rather than rely on attention alone. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 17. Same ARR, different burn

We have two startups in our portfolio in a similar category. They reached the same ARR, USD 4 million, in a similar amount of time. One burned USD 40 million, while the other burned USD 4 million. The difference in capital burned is striking even though the ARR and time to reach it are similar. VC pressure to break things and go fast does not always produce a great result. Source: anonymized portfolio comparison and opinion supplied by Piotr Karwatka for this keynote. Company identities and exact time periods have not been disclosed.

## 18. Another company in our portfolio

Here is another example from our portfolio. This startup had viral marketing, looked very cool and raised a large round. Two years later, the team pivoted and was looking from scratch for an entirely new direction. All three startups in these two examples are in our portfolio. This is another reason I see a large round as a commitment rather than proof of a durable business. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s anonymized portfolio account supplied for this keynote.

## 19. A customer willing to pay

We used to build companies where there was a customer willing to pay. Getting a customer to pay is harder than getting a VC to invest when the market is overheated. The difference matters: customer money comes from solving a problem someone values enough to buy. This is the kind of demand I want founders to build around. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 20. Paying customers and persistence

Build something customers will pay for. And choose something you can keep doing long enough that you do not give up. Startups die when the founder gives up. For me, that makes persistence part of the choice of what to build: a business needs customers, and the founder needs a reason to keep working on it. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s advice and perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 21. A market that can support you

Check whether the market gives you enough room to survive and earn as much as you need to earn. Building a company worth 100 million is a pretty good idea. It is worth choosing the size and kind of company you actually want to build and a market that can support it. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote. No currency has been specified for the 100 million valuation.

## 22. Time works in your favor

Time works in your favor. You make wiser decisions and watch costs more closely. You understand customers better. You hire more thoughtfully. The journey teaches you. These are advantages you can build as you keep working on a real business, learning from customers and living with your decisions. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s perspective supplied for this keynote.

## 23. The first customer

A useful pilot needs more than an enthusiastic introduction. It needs an owner who cares about the problem, a scope small enough to complete and a success condition the parties can review. Agree on access to users and the relevant environment. Find out who can authorize a wider rollout. If the pilot succeeds technically but nobody owns the next step, the team may still learn very little about adoption. The checklist is a proposal for structuring a conversation. Teams should adapt it to their product and customer constraints.

## 24. The next step

Build something. Try to sell it. Collect feedback and keep going. That is the action I want to leave you with. A useful first version, an attempt to sell it and real feedback give you something concrete to work with. Keep doing it. Source: Piotr Karwatka’s closing advice supplied for this keynote.
